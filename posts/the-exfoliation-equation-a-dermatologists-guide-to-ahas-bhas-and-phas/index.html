The Exfoliation Equation: A Dermatologist's Guide to AHAs, BHAs, and PHAs

2026-05-29 · Dermatology · Tags: The, dermatology

Exfoliation is one of the most misunderstood steps in a skincare routine. Some people treat it as a weekly ritual that promises instant glow, while others avoid it entirely for fear of damaging their skin. The truth lies somewhere in between. As a dermatologist, I've seen the transformative power of well-chosen exfoliants—and the havoc that overzealous or misinformed use can wreak. In this article, I'll break down the science behind chemical exfoliation, help you navigate the alphabet soup of AHAs, BHAs, and PHAs, and give you a practical framework to incorporate exfoliation safely and effectively into your own routine.

The Science of Skin Cell Turnover

Your skin is a dynamic organ, constantly renewing itself. The outermost layer, the stratum corneum, is composed of dead skin cells held together by a lipid matrix. Underneath, living keratinocytes are born in the basal layer and gradually migrate upward, maturing and eventually becoming corneocytes—the flat, dead cells that form the protective barrier. This entire journey takes about 28 to 30 days in a young adult, but as we age, the process slows down, sometimes doubling to 60 days or more by our 40s and 50s.

When cell turnover slows, dead skin cells accumulate on the surface, leading to a dull, rough complexion, clogged pores, and an uneven texture. This is where exfoliation steps in. By accelerating the removal of these dead cells, exfoliation reveals fresher, more radiant skin underneath. But not all exfoliation is created equal. Physical exfoliants—like scrubs and brushes—can cause micro-tears in the skin, especially when used aggressively. Chemical exfoliants, on the other hand, work by dissolving the bonds that hold dead cells together, allowing them to shed more gently and uniformly. This is why I almost always recommend chemical exfoliation over physical methods for most skin types.

Decoding the Alphabet: AHAs, BHAs, and PHAs

Chemical exfoliants are broadly categorized into three main types: alpha-hydroxy acids (AHAs), beta-hydroxy acids (BHAs), and polyhydroxy acids (PHAs). Each has unique properties that make them suitable for different skin concerns and types.

AHAs are water-soluble acids derived from sugary fruits, milk, or other sources. Common examples include glycolic acid (from sugarcane), lactic acid (from milk), and mandelic acid (from bitter almonds). AHAs work on the surface of the skin, breaking down the intercellular bonds between corneocytes. They are excellent for improving skin texture, fading hyperpigmentation, and boosting radiance. Because they are water-soluble, they are best suited for normal to dry skin types, as they can also help attract moisture to the skin.

BHAs are oil-soluble acids, with salicylic acid being the most well-known. Salicylic acid is derived from willow bark and has a unique ability to penetrate through the lipid-rich sebum that clogs pores. This makes it particularly effective for oily and acne-prone skin. BHAs also have anti-inflammatory properties, which can help calm active breakouts. They work both on the surface and inside the pore, making them ideal for blackheads, whiteheads, and congested skin.

PHAs are the newer, gentler cousins of AHAs. They have larger molecular structures, which means they penetrate the skin more slowly and are less irritating. Examples include gluconolactone and lactobionic acid. PHAs offer similar benefits to AHAs—exfoliation, hydration, and antioxidant properties—but with a much lower risk of irritation. They are perfect for sensitive skin, rosacea-prone skin, or those new to exfoliation.

Choosing the Right Exfoliant for Your Skin Type

There is no one-size-fits-all exfoliant. Your choice should be guided by your skin type, concerns, and tolerance. Here’s a practical breakdown:

  • Dry or mature skin: Opt for AHAs like lactic acid or mandelic acid. These are humectants, meaning they draw water into the skin, providing both exfoliation and hydration. Start with a low concentration (5-8%) and use 1-2 times per week.
  • Oily or acne-prone skin: BHAs, specifically salicylic acid, are your best friend. Look for a 2% concentration in a leave-on product. Use it 2-3 times per week, focusing on the T-zone and areas prone to congestion.
  • Sensitive or reactive skin: PHAs are your safest bet. They provide gentle exfoliation without the sting. You can also consider a low-strength lactic acid (5%) if you tolerate it. Always patch test first.
  • Combination skin: You may benefit from a combination approach—using a BHA on oily areas and an AHA on dry areas. Alternatively, choose a product that contains both, but start with a lower concentration to avoid irritation.

Remember, exfoliation is not a daily necessity. Over-exfoliating can strip the skin barrier, leading to redness, flaking, and increased sensitivity. The goal is to achieve a healthy glow, not to peel your skin off.

The Art of Frequency and Concentration

When it comes to exfoliation, more is not better. The key is to find the sweet spot that works for your skin. For most people, exfoliating 1-3 times per week is sufficient. If you're new to chemical exfoliants, start with once a week and gradually increase as your skin builds tolerance. Pay attention to how your skin feels—if it feels tight, itchy, or looks red, you're overdoing it.

Concentration matters just as much as frequency. Over-the-counter AHAs typically range from 5% to 15%, while BHAs are usually 0.5% to 2%. Higher concentrations are not necessarily better; they just increase the risk of irritation. For beginners, I recommend starting with the lowest effective concentration and working your way up. For example, a 5% glycolic acid toner is a great entry point, while a 10% glycolic acid serum is better suited for experienced users.

Another crucial point: always use exfoliants on clean, dry skin. Applying them to damp skin can increase penetration and irritation. And never combine multiple exfoliants in the same routine unless you're experienced and know your skin can handle it. If you're using a retinoid, be especially cautious—retinoids already accelerate cell turnover, so adding a strong exfoliant can lead to severe irritation.

Pairing Exfoliation with Other Actives

Exfoliation can enhance the efficacy of other skincare products by allowing them to penetrate more deeply. However, it also increases the skin's sensitivity, so you need to be strategic about what you pair it with.

In the morning, after exfoliating the night before, your skin is more vulnerable to UV damage. This is non-negotiable: you must wear a broad-spectrum sunscreen with at least SPF 30 every day. Exfoliation removes the outer layer of dead cells that provide some natural protection, so your skin is more exposed to UV rays. Skipping sunscreen is the fastest way to undo the benefits of exfoliation and accelerate aging.

When it comes to other actives, vitamin C is a great companion to exfoliation, but not on the same day. Use vitamin C in the morning and exfoliate in the evening, or alternate days. Niacinamide, on the other hand, is very compatible with exfoliants and can help soothe and strengthen the skin barrier. Hyaluronic acid is also a good addition, as it provides hydration that exfoliation can deplete.

Avoid using exfoliants on the same day as strong retinoids (like tretinoin) or benzoyl peroxide. If you're using a prescription retinoid, consider exfoliating on your “off” nights, or skip exfoliation altogether if your skin is already sensitive. The goal is to achieve synergy, not to overwhelm your skin.

Common Exfoliation Mistakes and How to Fix Them

Even with the best intentions, mistakes happen. Here are the most common pitfalls I see in my practice, and how to correct them:

  • Over-exfoliating: Using too many exfoliating products or using them too frequently. Fix: Cut back to once a week and give your skin a break. Focus on barrier repair with ceramides and soothing ingredients.
  • Using physical and chemical exfoliants together: This is a recipe for irritation. Fix: Choose one method and stick with it. If you love your scrub, use it sparingly and avoid chemical exfoliants on the same day.
  • Ignoring your skin's signals: If your skin stings, burns, or feels tight after exfoliation, you're doing too much. Fix: Stop exfoliating for a week, then reintroduce at a lower frequency or concentration.
  • Not using sunscreen: As mentioned, this is critical. Fix: Make sunscreen a non-negotiable part of your morning routine, even on cloudy days.
  • Exfoliating active breakouts: If you have open wounds or severe acne, exfoliating can worsen inflammation. Fix: Treat active breakouts with spot treatments and wait until the skin heals before resuming exfoliation.

Remember, exfoliation is a tool, not a punishment. It should feel comfortable and leave your skin looking and feeling better, not worse.

Conclusion

Exfoliation is a powerful ally in your skincare arsenal, but it demands respect and understanding. By choosing the right type of exfoliant for your skin, starting with low concentrations, and listening to your skin's feedback, you can achieve a smoother, brighter, and more even complexion without compromising your barrier. The key is to think of exfoliation as a delicate balancing act—one that, when done correctly, yields remarkable results. As always, if you're unsure about what's best for your skin, consult a board-certified dermatologist who can guide you based on your unique needs. Your skin is your largest organ; treat it with the care it deserves.
